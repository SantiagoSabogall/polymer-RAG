# Improved barrier properties of biodegradable PBAT films for packaging applications using EVOH: Morphology, permeability, biodegradation, and mechanical properties

## Mehdi Elhamnia1,2 | Ghodratollah Hashemi Motlagh1,2 | Seyed Hassan Jafari1

- 1Advanced Polymer Materials and Processing Lab (APMP), School of Chemical Engineering, College of Engineering, University of Tehran, Tehran, Iran
- 2Azmoon Dana Plastic Co., Polymer Testing &amp; Research Lab, Tehran, Iran

Seyed Hassan Jafari, Advanced Polymer Materials and Processing Lab (APMP), School of Chemical Engineering, College of Engineering, University of Tehran, Tehran, Iran. Email: email@example.com

Abstract

Oxygen transmission rate (OTR) and water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) of poly(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) (PBAT) were improved by poly(ethylene-co-vinyl alcohol) (EVOH). PBAT/EVOH blends at varying amounts of EVOH were prepared through melt mixing and characterized by OTR, WVTR, SEM, DSC, FTIR, biodegradation in soil, hydrolytic degradation, and tensile tests. EVOH considerably improved the barrier properties of PBAT, example, 77% reduction in OTR and 40% reduction in WVTR at 50 wt% EVOH. Four models of Maxwell, Higuchi, Chiew &amp; Glandt, and Levy were used to evaluate morphology and mechanism of transmission. A high elongation at break of 730% was observed for PBAT/EVOH blends up to 20 wt% EVOH but after that it severely dropped to 350% due to limited compatibility between the two polymers. Limited hydrogen bonding between PBAT and EVOH was observed by FTIR. EVOH accelerated the hydrolytic degradation of PBAT due to its high-water absorption capacity. This was not observed for biodegradation in soil suggesting that EVOH may retard microbial activity. After 540 h of hydrolytic degradation, the melting point and crystallization temperature of remaining PBAT increased by 5 and 10 C due to more degradation in adipate rubbery domains and less degradation in terephthalate hard segments.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The disposal of plastic waste particularly in the packaging industry is considered to be a serious environmental issue, embossing the necessity to find proper alternatives to replace commodity non-biodegradable polymers such as polyethylene, PET and PVC. Poly(butylene adipate-coterephthalate) (PBAT) is a biodegradable copolymer with excellent processing and mechanical properties, most importantly with a balanced flexibility-strength due to its

aliphatic-aromatic structure. PBAT is being used mainly for packaging and agricultural applications.1 Thus, PBAT is a good replacement for polyethylene except for its higher water and oxygen permeability. Therefore, its high gas and water vapor permeability has restricted its application in packaging industry.

There are two main approaches to improve the barrier properties of PBAT. Some studies have added nanoparticles to polymer matrix to improve the barrier properties of PBAT, for example, PBAT/cellulose nano-

crystal (CNC),2 PBAT/silver nano-particle (AgNPs),3 PBAT/ZnO,4 PBAT/nanoclay,5,6 and PBAT/graphene oxide nano-sheet (GONS).7 In fact, spherical or platelet nano-particles lengthen the path of permeation for gas molecules. Among them a best result of 70% reduction in gas permeability was reported. However, nanoparticles are expensive, hard to disperse and usually cause severe deterioration in polymer flexibility and toughness. In the second approach high barrier polymers are usually used in a multilayer structure to improve the barrier properties of the main polymer. Pan et al. used biodegradable PPC to improve mechanical and barrier properties of PBAT. They reported oxygen permeability coefficient (OPC) of 0.73 and 0.13 barrer for PBAT and PPC respectively. They showed that the addition of 20 wt% of PPC to PBAT decreases its OPC by 60%. Also, they observed that the biodegradation rate of PBAT increases with the presence of PPC.8 Touchaleaume et al. compared three biodegradable blends of PBAT/PLA, PBAT/PPC, and PBAT/starch with pure polyethylene. They showed that these PBAT blends are suitable alternatives over polyethylene in agricultural mulch films. However, the water vapor permeability of the PBAT blends were higher than polyethylene,9 a property which is very important feature for mulch films. In two other reports, PBAT was blended with non-biodegradable poly(hydroxy amino ether), PHAE,10 and poly(hydroxy ether of bisphenol A), PH,11,12 to improve the PBAT properties for packaging applications. The barrier properties of the blends were considerably improved. They reported 86% and 94% decrease in oxygen permeability with the addition of 25 wt% PHAE and 50 wt% PHAE.10 Also, up to 90% reduction in oxygen permeability of PBAT was observed by incorporating 25% PH.12 These observations were attributed to two reasons: creating a tortuous path, and reducing the excess free volume due to strong polymer-polymer interactions which hinder solubility and diffusion of the penetrant.10,12 Later, they measured the oxygen permeability of the polymers and blends at 45% relative humidity. They found that the oxygen permeability of pure polymers did not change considerably, but it noticeably increased for all the blends which suggested that water molecules can interact with the polymers, decrease polymer-polymer interactions, and in consequence increase free volume.12

Poly(ethylene-co-vinyl alcohol), EVOH, is well known for its very low gas permeability with a unique role in packaging industry.13 It has been frequently used in polyethylene and polypropylene as the main materials for packaging to improve their barrier properties14–16 A PP/EVOH 80/20 blend showed 24% reduction in OTR which was increased to 90% reduction using a compatibilizer.17 Recently, biaxially stretched polyamide 6/EVOH films were developed by Z. In applications where barrier properties are controlled by a dispersed phase, a higher aspect ratio of the dispersed phase and the creation of a three-dimensional network can further reduce the permeability, since the tortuosity and length of the permeate path increases.24,25 EVOH is not a biodegradable polymer, but it has been used with many biodegradable polymers like poly(lactic acid) (PLA),26,27 poly(caprolactone) (PCL),28 and poly(propylene carbonate) (PPC)29 to improve their barrier properties. Lee et al.26 synthesized EVOH-graft-PLA blend by means of reactive mixing in the presence of a catalyst. They showed that by grafting EVOH and PLA the domain size of EVOH decreased while mechanical properties had an increment. Also, they observed that the biodegradation of EVOH-g-PLA took place slower than the EVOH/PLA simple blend and more slowly than that of pure PLA. Gui et al.27 in 2013 added EVOH to PLA with the goal of improving PLA barrier properties. They found that the oxygen and water vapor permeability of PLA linearly decreased by the addition of EVOH compositions, so that, adding 50 wt% of EVOH reduced the oxygen and water vapor transmission by 68% and 60%, respectively. Also, they investigated the mechanical properties and biodegradability of the blends and found that

the presence of EVOH can promote the toughness and biodegradation of PLA.

EVOH is an available, commercial, and inexpensive polymer with excellent barrier properties which can enhance the barrier properties of PBAT for packaging applications. Thus, studying the properties of PBAT/ EVOH blends will be very useful to explore the potential advantages along with the challenges and drawbacks.30 There are very limited reports on PBAT/EVOH blends namely our previous work on their compatibility, morphology, and interfacial tension. It was found that PBAT/EVOH is an incompatible blend with mechanical properties below mixture law. An interfacial tension of 2.5 mN m 1 was obtained for PBAT/EVOH blends.30 The aim of this work is to improve the barrier properties of PBAT through the addition of EVOH. However, the effects of EVOH on other properties of PBAT such as mechanical properties, biodegradation, crystallinity, and processing are important to be studied. In this work PBAT was blended with 0, 10, 20, 30, and 50 wt% EVOH and oxygen and water permeability of the resulting blends were measured. The morphology, tensile properties, crystallinity, biodegradation in soil, and hydrolytic degradation of the blends were

investigated to evaluate the cost of permeability improvement for these properties.

## 2 | EXPERIMENTAL 2.1 | Material and blending

PBAT, Ecoflex blend F C1200 from BASF Germany with melt flow rate of 2.7 g/10 min (190 C, 2.16 kg) and density of 1.26 g/cc was used. EVOH, EVAL F101B with ethylene content of 32 mol%, melt flow rate of 1.6 g/10 min (190 C, 2.16 kg) and density of 1.19 g/cc was supplied by Kuraray Belgium. The blends with 10, 20, 30, and 50 wt% of EVOH were prepared by a 60 cm3 internal melt mixer at a rotor speed of 90 rpm at 190 C for 15 min. Before mixing, the entire materials were dried in vacuum oven at 60 C for 24 h. The mixing conditions were selected according to our previous work.30 It should be noted that PBAT is thermal sensitive. But in our previous work,30 it was shown by DSC and rheometry analysis that PBAT is thermally stable at temperatures of 190 and 200 C for up to 30 min. In this work, the viscosity of PBAT was measured over time at 190 C to evaluate its thermal stability.

The result is presented in supplementary data (Figure 1S) showing nearly constant viscosity over time and therefore thermal stability of PBAT. The pure PBAT and EVOH were also processed with the same conditions as the base materials for making comparisons.

## 2.2 | Morphology

To observe the morphology of the blends, field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), NOVA NanoSEM 450, FEI Co., America at 5 KV voltage was used. The samples were cryo-fractured in liquid nitrogen and spotterd by gold before imaging. The obtained SEM images were also used to calculate mean average diameter of droplet size of EVOH disperse phase in the blends.

## 2.3 | FTIR test

ATR-FTIR analysis was used for PBAT, EVOH, and PBAT/EVOH 80/20 by a TENSOR 27, BRUKER FTIR, Germany, 4000 to 400 cm 1 at a resolution of 1 cm 1 to investigate interactions between the two polymers. The samples were compression-molded into films with a thickness of about 100 microns at temperature of 190 C and pressure of 100 bar.

## 2.4 | Tensile properties

Tensile properties of the blends were measured by a 20 kN universal tensile testing machine, JJ Test, China. After drying the blends in vacuum oven at 60 C for 24 h, a sheet of each blend with 1 mm in thickness was prepared by a hot press at 200 C and 100 bar with 1 min preheating and 2 min under full pressure. Then the mold was taken out from the press and cooled in ambient. Dog-bone shape samples were punched from these sheets. The measurements were carried out at 20 mm.min 1, 23 C and each reported value is an average of at least five specimens.

## 2.5 | OTR &amp; WVTR measurements

The blends were dried in a vacuum oven at 60 C for 24 h and then were molded into films with an average thickness of 60 μm. ## 2.6 | Biodegradation and hydrolytic degradation

Two methods were employed to investigate the biodegradability of the blends. (i) Burying in soil,

(ii) hydrolytic degradation in NaOH solution. The soil was prepared from campus of University of Tehran. Thin films with thicknesses between 160 and 190 μm were prepared as described in Section 2.4. The specimens were cut in 10 10 mm and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 C for 24 h before weighting. The prepared samples were buried in the soil and kept at 37 C. The moisture of soil was frequently checked to keep at 50 wt%. After certain times, the specimens were removed and washed with distilled water. Next, they were dried in vacuum oven at 60 C for 24 h prior weight measurement. Three specimens were weighted for each reported value.

Hydrolytic degradation of the blends was performed in 0.1 M NaOH solution, pH = 13 at 37 C. Thin films were prepared in the same way explained above. Each specimen was placed in a 15 cc falcon and 13 mL NaOH solution was added. The level of solution was periodically checked. If solution level dropped, distilled water was added. At certain times, the specimens were removed from the solution and washed. Then, they were dried in vacuum oven at 60 C for 24 h and then weighted. Three replicates were done for each reported value.

## 2.7 | Water absorption

The blends were molded into tablets of 25 mm in diameter and 2 mm in thickness at 190 C and dried in vacuum oven at 60 C for 24 h. Next, each tablet was weighted and placed in distilled water at room temperature. At subsequent times, the samples were removed and their surface was dried with a cellulose filter paper. The weight of the samples was recorded and they were returned into water for next measurements.

## 2.8 | Thermal analysis &amp; degradation

The effect of hydrolytic degradation on the crystallinity of the samples was investigated by differential scanning calorimetry, ZF-DSC-D2, Zufa industry Co., Shanghai, China, under dry nitrogen atmosphere. For each measurement, about 20 mg of the sample was placed in an aluminum pan and heated from room temperature to 220 C with a constant rate of 10 C/min and cooled to room temperature. The samples had the same thermal history because they were cut from the same film. DSC analysis at similar conditions were done up to a high temperature of 500 C to investigate thermal degradation, under nitrogen atmosphere, and thermal-oxidative degradation, under oxygen atmosphere, of PBAT and its blends. The runs show the temperatures at which thermal or thermal-oxidative degradation kick off.

## 3 | RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

## 3.1 | Morphology

The morphology of the blends through FE-SEM images are shown in Figure 1. A smooth surface is observed for pure PBAT and EVOH. By the addition of 10, 20, and 30 wt% EVOH, dispersed droplets of EVOH develop in the PBAT matrix which shows that these blends are not miscible. At 50 wt% EVOH, co-continuous morphology shows up. Number average diameter (dn) and volume average diameter (dv) of EVOH droplets were calculated by Equation 4 and listed in Table 2. The size of EVOH droplets in the blends was measured by Image J software and at least 200 droplets were measured for each reported mean value. As seen the droplet size is near submicron which could be an indication for the partial miscibility or compatibility of PBAT and EVOH.29 In the blends the droplet size of EVOH decreases when EVOH increases from 10 wt% to 20 wt% but then increases when EVOH increases from 20 wt% to 30 wt%. Both break-up and coalescence mechanisms occur in blending of immiscible polymer blends. A higher viscous matrix applies higher stress on droplets leading to more break-up while higher concentration of droplets causes more coalescence. As seen in supplementary data (Figure 2S), EVOH is much more viscous than PBAT. Therefore, at 20 wt% EVOH the effect of increased viscosity on break-up is more significant than that at 10 wt% EVOH while coalescence would be more significant at 30 wt% EVOH.

## 3.2 | FTIR results

The ATR-FTIR spectra for PBAT, EVOH, and PBAT/ EVOH 80/20 (Figure 3S) show characteristic peaks at 2934, 2862, and 1578 cm 1 attributed to asymmetric and symmetric stretching of C H bonds, and C C stretching in aromatic groups, respectively. Figure 2 represents selected ranges of spectrum for O H and C O groups to investigate inter-molecular interactions between PBAT and EVOH. Figure 2a,b show O H characteristic peaks around 3400 for stretching and 1300 for bending modes which are

stronger for EVOH rather than PBAT due to considerably lower number of O H groups located at the end of PBAT chains. The shifts in O H stretching and bending peaks in the blend show effective interactions between O H groups of EVOH and O H groups of PBAT. Similar results were reported by G. Z. Papageorgiou et al..31 In the opposite, Figure 2c does not show any significant shift for C O peak of PBAT in the blend. It can be concluded that there are not strong interactions between ester groups of PBAT and hydroxyl groups of EVOH.

## 3.3 | Thermal and thermal-oxidative stability

Figures 3 shows thermal degradation and thermal oxidative degradation of PBAT, EVOH and their blend. Here it is of more interest to see the effect of EVOH on

the thermal or thermal-oxidative degradation of PBAT. Figure 3a gives a thermal degradation temperature of around 435 C for PBAT, 347 C for EVOH and a bimodal of 393 and 428 C for PBAT/EVOH. As seen, PBAT is more thermally stable in the absence of oxygen which could be due to the presence of aromatic structure in the main chain of PBAT while EVOH is totally aliphatic. It is interesting to see that blending has increased the thermal degradation temperature of EVOH phase by about 40 C and has slightly decreased that of PBAT phase. Thermal degradation is endothermic.

Figure 3b shows DSC runs under oxygen atmosphere in which degradation is assigned via exothermic peaks. It is interesting to see two-step degradations for PBAT, EVOH and their blend. The degradation peaks occur around 360 and 480 C for PBAT, 370 and 470 C for EVOH and 360 C, 390 and 490 C for PBAT/EVOH

blend. As seen, the first degradation temperature of PBAT is lower than EVOH saying that PBAT is more sensitive to undergo thermal-oxidative degradation while the second peak of PBAT occurs at higher temperature in regards to that of EVOH saying that PBAT aromatic structure may play a role here. Surprisingly, thermal-oxidative temperatures of the blend are higher than those of either PBAT or EVOH. EVOH particles act as barriers against oxygen diffusion into the sample leading to a delay in oxidative degradation of PBAT. On the other hand, EVOH droplets are surrounded by PBAT phase, therefore, a delay in oxygen diffusion into EVOH droplets may occur. Similar observations were reported by S. Jafari et al.32 where they investigated the thermal and thermal-oxidative degradation of PET/PLA/GO composites. They observed that although graphene oxide accelerates thermal degradation of the composite under nitrogen, it retards its thermaloxidative degradation due to its barrier effect against oxygen diffusion.

In general, it can be concluded that the addition of EVOH to PBAT do not decrease the thermal-oxidative or thermal stability of PBAT which is in agreement with our previous results.30

## 3.4 | Mechanical properties

- Figure 4 shows tensile results of neat PBAT, EVOH, and their blends. The stress–strain curve of EVOH is shown separately. EVOH strength is much higher than PBAT while its elongation at break is much lower. As demonstrated in the figure, the blends with 10 and 20 wt% of EVOH closely retained the flexibility and toughness of PBAT although their tensile strength slightly reduced below PBAT. By increasing the EVOH content to 30 wt%, the mechanical properties dropped severely. The tensile strength of PBAT/EVOH 50/50 blend increased as compared to that of PBAT/EVOH 70/30 due to the continuity of EVOH phase, and elongation at break drastically dropped. Also, it should be noted that the strain hardening behavior of PBAT has disappeared for PBAT/EVOH 70/30 and PBAT/EVOH 50/50 blends, therefore these blends seem to be inappropriate for packaging and agricultural film applications. These observations are an indication of partial compatibility between PBAT and EVOH since the addition of 20 wt% EVOH do not considerably deteriorate the elongation at break of PBAT. However, the high strength of EVOH is not reflected in the blend which suggests a negative deviation from mixture rule saying that PBAT/EVOH is not well compatible.30

3.5 | Oxygen permeability

The oxygen permeability is of great importance in packaging applications, since it plays a fundamental role in food preservation. Foods can deteriorate as a result of oxidation reaction. The oxygen also enables the growth of aerobic microorganisms.33 The presence of water can change the permeability to oxygen; therefore, the oxygen permeability was measured in both dry and humid conditions.

The oxygen permeability of pure polymers and their blends at two relative humidities are presented in

- Figure 5. The oxygen permeability coefficient (OPC) of neat PBAT was 0.246 barrer at 0% RH and 25 C. This value was much lower, 0.000122 barrer20 for EVOH, and much higher 3 barrer21,22 for LDPE. As seen in Figure 5, oxygen permeability of PBAT steadily decreases with the addition of EVOH, e.g., 31% and 77% reduction in OPC at 20 wt% and 50 wt% EVOH.

EVOH acts as an impermeable particle against oxygen. Therefore, increasing the permeation path is the main reason for the permeability reduction. The co-continues morphology of PBAT/EVOH 50/50 could be more efficient to decrease OPC.34

The oxygen permeability of EVOH has strongly increased from 1.22 10 4 to 1.83 10 3 barrer, as the RH has increased from 0% to 95%. This change is not recognized in Figure 5. The hydroxyl groups in EVOH chains can create hydrogen bonding with water. Thus, water acts as a plasticizer and weakens strong interchain forces of EVOH.13,14 Figure 5 shows that the

increase in RH from 0% to 95% has slightly increased the OPC of PBAT and blends. In opposite to severe influence of humidity on gas permeability of EVOH, no considerable change was seen for the PBAT/EVOH blends. This can be affected by two reasons. Firstly, PBAT can prohibit water molecules to reach EVOH domains. Secondly, EVOH has much lower oxygen permeability than PBAT even at 95% RH, therefore the increased permeability of EVOH as a result of increased humidity is not reflected in the high permeability value of the blends.

## 3.6 | Water vapor permeability

Reduction in water vapor permeability can avoid fresh foods from drying and reduces water evaporation in agricultural lands. Therefore, water vapor permeability also plays an important role in packaging and agricultural mulch.

The water vapor permeability coefficients (WVPC) of the film of blends are shown in Figure 7. The obtained value of WVPC is 13.97 10 11 g.m.m 2.s 1.Pa 1 for

neat PBAT which is close to the reported value by others.3,12 This value shows a high water vapor permeability as compared to that of low density polyethylene, 0.07 10 11 g.m.m 2.s 1.Pa 1,20,23 as a common polymer in packaging and agricultural mulch. Therefore, to use PBAT in place of polyethylene, lower water vapor permeability is also required. In other word polyethylene suffers from only gas permeability while PBAT suffers from both water and gas permeability.

Although EVOH is known as a high gas barrier material, its water vapor permeability 0.30 10 11 g.m.m 2. s 1.Pa 1, is not as good as LDPE but still much better than that of PBAT. Figure 7 shows that 20 wt% of EVOH can reduce the water permeability of PBAT by 19%. The EVOH domains perform like a block and increase path tortuosity for penetrant molecules. Similar results were reported by Z. Gui et al.27 for PLA/EVOH blends.

## 3.7 | Biodegradability 3.7.1 | Hydrolytic degradation

Hydrolysis and microbial activity are two substantial processes that cause biodegradation. Hydrolysis is one of the initial processes of biodegradation.36 The hydrolytic degradation of PBAT and its blends with EVOH were studied by means of visual evaluation, weight loss, and DSC. In order to accelerate the test in a practical time range, the hydrolytic degradation was done in 0.1 M NaOH aqueous solution at 37 C.

Figure 8 shows the samples before and after the hydrolytic degradation at several times. It was found that PBAT/EVOH 50/50 became brittle after 300 h and lost its integrity after 420 h. PBAT/EVOH 70/30 blend showed brittleness after 540 h. Only in the case of neat EVOH no distinguished change was observed.

Weight change of the films versus time in the NaOH solution is illustrated in Figure 9. The weight loss starts after 180 h which is due to the diffusion in and out of molecules and the time needed for the reaction to occur enough to see measurable weight change. 16.7% of neat PBAT is lost after 540 h while neat EVOH presents no changes. Interestingly, an increase in the weight loss of PBAT is observed after blending with EVOH. This phenomenon could be due to two reasons. First, EVOH is highly water absorbent and its presence leads to an increment in hydrophilicity of the films. As a result, water molecules penetrate into the films more easily and accelerate the hydrolysis of the ester bonds of PBAT. Figure 10 displays the increase of water absorption in the presence of EVOH. Second, hydroxyl groups in EVOH chains can act as a catalyst and cause the ester

bonds to break. However, it is not known which of these two factors is more effective.

The ester bonds of PBAT chains during hydrolytic degradation break down which causes the melting point to decrease. It is assumed that degradation mainly occurs in the amorphous regions of PBAT while here decrease in melting point and in contrary increase in crystallization of PBAT were observed. The soft aliphatic butylene adipate (BA) units in PBAT are infused into the hard aromatic butylene terephthalate (BT) crystal lattice.36 Hydrolytic degradation reactions could easily attack amorphous parts, though water penetration to crystalline sections is more difficult. Hence, BA units are more vulnerable to biodegradation.37 As most of BA units degrade which are rubbery part and prohibit crystallization, BT segments can start crystallization at higher temperatures without the

negative effect of BA segments.11,37 The crystallization enthalpy decreases with hydrolytic degradation due to the fact that degradation deteriorates the pristine crystalline structure of PBAT and causes defects and imperfections in the crystalline regions that reform during the cooling cycle.

## 3.7.2 | Biodegradation in soil

Biodegradability of PBAT and its blends with EVOH in soil was investigated. Samples were buried in the form of films into the soil and their appearance and weight loss were evaluated after removing them from the soil at various times. Figure 12 shows considerable color change over time. This change in color stems from the microbial activity. Figure 13 shows that the weight changes for all samples are not much different saying that they are not much different in biodegradation. After 250 days, about 20% weight loss was observed for all samples while our visual examination showed sever drop in flexibility. As mentioned before, biodegradability is a result of hydrolysis and microbial activity. In Section 3.7.1 we observed acceleration of PBAT hydrolytic degradation by the addition of EVOH. Therefore, it can be concluded that with

increasing EVOH content, the microbial activity is suppressed. Also, by comparing the images of PBAT/EVOH 50/50 and pure PBAT samples after 250 days (in Figure 12), it can be seen that the color change for PBAT is significantly more than that for PBAT/EVOH

50/50. This observation confirms the hypothesis which EVOH decreases the microbial activity and increases the hydrolytic degradation. Therefore, EVOH addition can be used as a tool to manipulate the biodegradation and life time of PBAT films.

## 4 | CONCLUSIONS

PBAT/EVOH blends were prepared via melt mixing at 0, 10, 20, 30, and 50 wt% EVOH compositions with the aim of improving water and oxygen barrier properties of PBAT. A droplet-matrix morphology was observed up to 30 wt% EVOH but co-continuity developed at 50 wt% EVOH. The size of EVOH droplets was about 1 micron suggesting partial compatibility between EVOH and PBAT. PBAT/EVOH (80/20) was considered as the optimum blend composition.

FTIR results confirmed significant hydrogen bonding between hydroxyl groups of EVOH and the hydroxyl groups of PBAT. However, this hydrogen bonding cannot make a strong interaction between PBAT and EVOH due to a very low number of hydroxyl groups in PBAT.

The results of thermal and thermal-oxidative degradation analysis by DSC showed that the addition of EVOH to PBAT does not accelerate degradation of PBAT which is a concern during melt mixing of the two polymers. Surprisingly EVOH can even improve the thermal-oxidative stability of PBAT.

The addition of EVOH with much higher tensile strength of near 90 MPa could not increase the tensile strength of PBAT in the blends, about 40 MPa, saying that these blends are not highly compatible. However, the high elongation at break of PBAT, about 800%, did not severely affected by EVOH up to 20 wt% again saying that these blends are partially compatible. The addition of EVOH even at low contents suppressed the excellent strain hardening behavior of PBAT.

In general, water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) and oxygen transmission rate (OTR) of the blends were

decreased with EVOH content. A 77% decrease in OTR and a 40% reduction in WVTR were observed at the highest content of EVOH (50 wt%) as compared to those of PBAT. The WVTR and OTR of PBAT were decreased by 19% and 31% at 20 wt% EVOH without considerable scarification of PBAT toughness. Maxwell, Higuchi, and Chiew &amp; Glandt models well fitted the OTR data up to 30 wt% EVOH for blends with droplet-matrix morphology. These models, adequate for droplet-matrix morphologies, could not predict the observed negative deviation of OTR for PBAT/EVOH 50/50 co-continuous blend. However, Levy model which does not consider disperse morphology predicted the negative deviation. Therefore, the models can differentiate between matrix-droplet and co-continuous morphology.

Hydrolytic degradation of the blends in 0.1 M NaOH solution showed that EVOH accelerates the degradation of PBAT due to the higher absorption capacity of EVOH and possibly the catalytic function of EVOH in the breakage of ester group of PBAT. Along with hydrolytic degradation the melting point of PBAT decreased but in contrary its crystallization point increased. Hydrolytic degradation mainly occurs in the amorphous regions and in rubbery butylene adipate segments which are retarders for the crystallization of butylene adipate segments.

Biodegradation in soil at 37 C and 50 wt% moisture showed that all blends except pure EVOH undergo biodegradation by about 20 wt% reduction after 250 days. The high water absorption capacity of EVOH did not accelerate the degradation as occurred in hydrolytic degradation suggesting that EVOH may prevent microbial activity for PBAT biodegradation.

The present paper is a first step toward using PBAT/ EVOH films for food packaging and agricultural applications especially, instead of polyethylene single or multilayer films. Further research is being done in our group in several ways: compatibilization of these blends, multilayer films of PBAT/EVOH and addition of nanoparticles such as GO and ZnO. To impart barrier effect toward moisture it is required to add another suitable polymer such as polyethylene. PBAT/EVOH alone is not adequate for moisture impermeability since both polymers have high moisture absorption or transmission rate.
