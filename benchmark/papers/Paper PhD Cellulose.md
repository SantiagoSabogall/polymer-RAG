## A B S T R A C T

Growing demand for sustainable packaging materials has intensified interest in polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) as  biodegradable alternatives to plastics. Among these, poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyvalerate) (PHBV), a  commercially available copolymer, shows promise for packaging but is hindered by limited mechanical strength  and poor moisture resistance. We explore a biotechnological strategy to enhance PHBV film performance using  two additives produced via microbial fermentation of effluents from cacao fruit processing: (i) TEMPO-oxidized  bacterial cellulose (BC-TOCN) chemically modified by amidation with octadecyl amine (BC-TOCN-AMD C-18),  and (ii) low-molecular-weight polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB). Hydrophobization of BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 was  confirmed by a maximum water contact angle of 147◦, higher than unmodified BC-TOCN, and decreased surface  free energy (SFE) with minimal polar contribution.

Incorporating the two additives into PHBV films formed by melt extrusion increased the water contact angle  from 89 to 112◦, enhanced tensile strength from 0.50 to 5.5 MPa, and improved surface smoothness. Additionally, the films show 10.47 % reduction in water permeability and 9.54 % decrease in oxygen permeability  compared to neat PHBV. These effects are attributed to synergistic interactions promoting improved dispersion  and interfacial compatibility. The results highlight the potential of BC-based nanomaterials and homopolymeric  PHB to modulate PHBV properties, supporting applications in sustainable packaging requiring moisture control  and biodegradability.

Poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) (PHB) is the most widely studied PHA. It is  a highly crystalline and brittle polymer with a melting point similar to  that of polypropylene (Sharma et al., 2021). However, its brittleness and  slow crystallisation hinder processing, prompting interest in copolymers  such as poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyvalerate) (PHBV), which  offers increased flexibility and is produced on an industrial scale  (El-Hadi et al., 2002; Policastro et al., 2021). Nevertheless, PHBV exhibits mechanical fragility, a narrow processing window, and sensitivity  to gas and moisture transmission, limiting its full replacement of synthetic plastics (Bossu et al., 2021). To overcome these drawbacks, the  use of additives like inorganic nanoparticles, carbon fibers, and cellulose  has been explored (Ashori et al., 2019;  Aydemir  &amp;  Gardner, 2022;  Díez-Pascual &amp; Díez-Vicente, 2014). Bacterial cellulose (BC), which is  produced by  Gluconacetobacter  spp., is highly crystalline, pure, and  biocompatible (Barud et al., 2011; French, 2017). In packaging applications,  BC  and  other  cellulose  forms  have  improved  nucleation,  strength, and barrier properties in PHA films (Martínez-Sanz et al.,  2014; Qasim et al., 2020). However, due to its hydrophilic nature, cellulose exhibits limited compatibility with the hydrophobic PHA matrix,  which may result in poor interfacial adhesion (Jo et al., 2021).

Worldwide, plastic use and production have reached levels that harm  environmental balance and, in turn, human health. According to the  FAO, the annual production of synthetic plastics ranges from 350 to 400  million tons, with 37.3 million tons used for food packaging (FAO, 2025;  Houssini et al., 2025). However, once these plastics serve their purpose,  82 million tons per year become waste, with 1 to 2 million tons ending  up in the oceans (Ritchie, 2023). This issue highlights the urgent need to  reevaluate and redesign packaging materials, focusing on minimizing  synthetic components and improving their recyclability (Costello et al.,  2009;  Shen et al., 2020). In response, biodegradable polymers have  become a promising alternative to reduce the environmental impact of  oil-based plastics. Among them, polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) stand  out for their efficient biodegradability and adjustable physicochemical  properties, which depend on the chemical structure of their repetitive  polymer unit. (Khanna &amp; Srivastava, 2005; Tan et al., 2014).

This work presents a novel approach that combines: (i) BC (synthesized from CME) modified via surface amidation, (ii) PHB synthesized  from CME, and (iii) a commercial PHBV matrix, to fabricate biodegradable films via extrusion. We hypothesize that bacterial cellulose  nanofibers amidated with octadecylamine (BC-TOCN-AMD C-18) could  enhance the mechanical and barrier properties of PHBV or PHBV/PHB  blends  by  improving  matrix–filler  compatibility.  To  test  this,  BC‑TOCN‑AMD C-18 was characterized using XRD, SEM, FTIR, TGA,  elemental analysis, titration, ζ‑potential, and CA. Four film types were  fabricated: neat PHBV, PHBV/PHB, PHBV/BC-TOCN-AMD C-18, and a  ternary blend with PHBV/PHB/BC-TOCN-AMD C-18. The structural,  thermal,  and  physicochemical  characteristics  of  the  additives  and  polymeric films were measured, and the results indicate significant potential for utilizing cellulose-based additives and low-molecular-weight  PHB to modulate the biodegradable films’ performance.

Several studies have incorporated unmodified or only physically  treated cellulose into PHB or PHBV matrices to improve film properties.  For instance, Qasim et al. (2020) a 32 % tensile strength increase was  reported in PHB films with 2.5 wt % cellulose, while 5 wt % microcellulose in PHBV was found to improve the barrier properties. Ding  et al. (2021) also observed increases in tensile strength and modulus,  attributed to polymer–fiber interactions. To enhance compatibility with  hydrophobic biopolymer matrices, various surface modification strategies have been explored to reduce the inherent hydrophilicity of cellulose. Techniques such as esterification with organic acids, amination,  and surface amidation have been widely documented for tuning cellulose surface chemistry and improving interfacial adhesion (Peng et al.,  2016; Sirvio et al., 2016¨ ; Taubner et al., 2013). However, only a few  studies report the incorporation of chemically modified BC into PHB or  PHBV matrices, particularly when both components are derived from  agro-industrial residues, limiting the understanding of their synergistic  effects on film performance. Our previous studies have shown that  amidation  of  microcrystalline  cellulose  (Gomez ´ et  al.,  2017)  and  TEMPO-oxidized nanocellulose (TOCN) using TBTU and long-chain  alkylamines (C12 and C18) reduces polarity and increases hydrophobicity.  Calderon-Vergara et al. (2020)´ reported a 73 % reduction in  carboxylate groups in TOCN and an increase in contact angle (CA) from  24◦ to 126◦, indicating potential for improved compatibility with hydrophobic polymers, such as PHAs.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Materials

2,2,6,6-Tetramethylpiperidin-1-oxyl (TEMPO,  ≥98 %), octadecylamine (AMN C-18, ≥95 %), and [O-(1H-benzotriazol-1-yl)-N,N,N’,N’-  tetramethyluronium tetrafluoroborate] (TBTU, ≥98 %) were acquired  from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Sodium hypochlorite solution (NaClO, 15 %  w/v, commercial grade) was sourced locally (Santander, Colombia).  Sodium bromide (NaBr, ≥99 %), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, pellets, ≥98  %), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37 % w/w), ethanol (EtOH, 96 % v/v),  methanol (MeOH, reagent grade, ≥99 %), chloroform (CHCl₃, ≥99.8 %),  and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, anhydrous, 99.8 %) were purchased  from  Merck  (Germany).  PHBV  was  purchased  from  Danimer  Bioplastics®  (DAN 02,198-USA) with Mn:42.04 kDa and PDI = 1.10 as  determined by GPC (standard PS 2.5KDa-75 kDa) and Tm of 128–145 ◦C.

### 2.2. Bacterial cellulose biosynthesis

BC was biosynthesized by Gluconacetobacter xylinus using previously  established growth conditions: 15 days of incubation at 30 ◦C, pH 6.0,  and cacao mucilage exudate (CME) supplemented and diluted 50 % as  the growth medium (Saavedra-Sanabria et al., 2021). The process was  carried out in a reactor with a working volume of 3 L, supplemented  with 1.5 L of cacao mucilage exudate, where BC was produced extracellularly as a thick film floating on the reactor’s surface. The BC film  was washed, sterilized, and stored in the refrigerator for further use.

In parallel, the use of agro-industrial residues as carbon sources for  microbial biopolymer production is gaining attention. Colombia’s cacao  industry generates large volumes of cacao mucilage exudate (CME), a  sugar-rich effluent. Our previous work demonstrated that CME can be  used to produce PHB and BC via microbial fermentation (Quintero-Silva  et al., 2023; Saavedra-Sanabria et al., 2021). This valorization supports  the circular economy and aligns with the Sustainable Development  Goals (SDGs): SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), by  reducing waste and promoting resource efficiency; and SDG 13 (Climate  Action), by replacing fossil-derived plastics and lowering greenhouse  gas  emissions.  These  goals  also  align  with  the  sustainable,  smart  manufacturing systems advocated by the innovation principles of Industry 4.0 and 5.0.

### 2.3. Polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) biosynthesis

PHB was biosynthesized using a native  Bacillus megaterium  (B2)  strain under previously established culture conditions (Quintero-Silva  et al., 2023). The bioprocess was conducted in a reactor containing 4 L of  a growth medium composed of cacao mucilage exudate (SCME), residual medium for BC production (RMBC), and basic salt medium (BSM) in  equal proportions (0.0.0.0::1), corresponding to 1333 mL of each. An  aerobic fermentation process was carried out at 34 ◦C ± 2 ◦C under  constant stirring at 200 rpm for 24 h. The PHB produced intracellularly  underwent an extraction process involving treatment with an aqueous  sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, 0.5 % w/v) solution and mechanical  disruption of the dried sediment. The obtained PHB was then purified by  washing with water and ethanol (H2O: EtOH) to remove impurities and  cell debris. Finally, the pure PHB was dried in an oven at 50 ◦C for 24 h.  The PHB exhibited Mn of 0.9 kDa and PDI of 1.0 per GPC (PS standard  2.5 kDa-75 kDa). The polymer particles displayed an average size of 1.45  µm, with a maximum particle size of 1.75 µm. Previous studies of the  same polymer reported Tm and Td of 165,6 and 270,6 ◦C, respectively.

Recent research has incorporated cellulose into PHBV matrices for  food packaging. For example, Yu et al. (2014) developed PHBV nanocomposites reinforced with methyl-esterified cellulose nanocrystals,  achieving enhanced mechanical strength, thermal stability, and barrier  properties without compatibilizers. Soares da Silva et al. (2023) produced a composite of plasticized BNC coated with PHBV showing  reduced water vapor permeability (0.990 to 0.032 g⋅μm⋅m⁻²⋅day⁻¹⋅Pa⁻¹)  and higher hydrophobicity (CA from 10 to 40◦ to 80–90◦). However,  most studies rely on commercial cellulose nanocrystals and physical  treatments  without  employing  specific  chemical  modifications  to  improve compatibility at the interface. Moreover, little research has  been conducted on combining chemically modified BC with biopolymer  matrices derived from agro-industrial residues.

### 2.4. TEMPO-catalyzed oxidation of BC

TEMPO (0.016 g) and NaBr (0.1 g) were added to a BC aqueous  suspension (1 % w/v). After dissolution, sodium hypochlorite (NaClO)  was added dropwise, maintaining a 1:6 molar ratio (cellulose: NaClO).  The reaction flask was then placed in an ultrasonic bath (Bransonic, 40  kHz, 130 W) for four hours, during which the pH was maintained at 10.5  by adding NaOH. The stabilization of the pH indicated the reaction  completion. At this point, ethanol was added to stop the catalytic  reaction cycle (Ovalle-Serrano et al., 2018). The reaction mixture was  centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 15 min, and the solid was washed with  water and 0.1 M HCl until a neutral pH was achieved (Habibi et al.,  2006). Finally, to obtain a uniform dispersion of TEMPO-oxidized bacterial cellulose nanofibers (BC-TOCN), an ultrasonic probe (Sonics  Vibra-Cell VC750, 20 kHz, 750 W) operated at 40 % amplitude with a  pulsed  1:1  duty  cycle  was  used  to  mechanically  disintegrate  the  BC-TOCN pellet for 10 min in an ice bath.

### 2.5. BC-TOCN surface modification

A solution of TBTU in dimethylformamide (DMF) was mixed with an  aqueous solution of BC-TOCN at a 2:1 molar ratio TBTU:BC-TOCN. The  reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 30 min, maintaining a basic pH. A primary amine solution in DMF (AMN C-18) was  added to the mixture, keeping a 4:1 molar ratio of amine groups to  carboxyl (COOH) units in BC-TOCN. The mixture was stirred continuously at room temperature for two hours. The amidated cellulose derivative floated to the surface of the reaction mixture aqueous solution  due to the increased hydrophobicity. The solution was then filtered and  washed with methanol, 0.01 M HCl, and water to remove unreacted  species (Gomez et al., 2017´ ). The resulting material was freeze-dried and  labeled as bacterial cellulose nanofibers amidated with octadecyl amine  (BC-TOCN-AMD C-18).

### 2.7. Characterization of cellulosic materials

The colloidal stability of BC-TOCN was assessed through ζ potential  measurements of 0.01 % w/v aqueous solutions, utilizing a Malvern  Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Worcestershire, UK) and a folded capillary cell.  Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was used to analyze the  chemical structures at each processing stage. IR spectra were recorded  with a Bruker Tensor 27 FT-IR spectrometer equipped with a Platinum  Diamond ATR unit A225/Q (Billerica, MA), covering the range of  4000–500 cm⁻¹, with a resolution of 2 cm⁻¹, 32 scans, and a scanning  speed of 2.2 kHz. Morphological changes and surface dimensions of BC-  TOCN and its derivatives were examined using a QUANTA FEG 650  scanning electron microscope (Oregon, USA) equipped with a wide-field  detector. The instrument was operated at a high vacuum with an  accelerating voltage of 30 kV. Samples were mounted on metal stubs  with carbon adhesive tape and coated with a gold layer using a Quorum  150T ES sputter coater (Oregon, USA).

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on an STA 449 F5  Jupiter instrument (S˜ao Paulo, Brazil), using nitrogen as the carrier gas  at a flow rate of 50 mL/min and a heating rate of 10 ◦C/min over a  temperature range of 25 to 500  ◦C. ### 2.8. Film preparation and optical characterization

Polymeric films, with and without the BC-based additive, were prepared by extruding pre-extruded pellets. A first extruder was equipped  with a mold for a thread with a diameter of 4 mm, and the second  extruder carried a lip-type model for film (thickness from 100 to 1000  µm and a width of 200 mm). The process was carried out using a  BimekTM BK30 (Bogota, Colombia) single-screw extruder featuring a  screw with a diameter of 2 inches and a length-to-diameter (L/D) ratio of  25, operating at a speed of 35 rpm. The thermal profile for pellet and  sheet extrusion was 170 ◦C, 185 ◦C, 200 ◦C, and 210 ◦C. The blends’  pellets were stored at 25 ◦C with 50 % relative humidity to prevent  hydrolysis. Before extruding 500 g of each blend, the material was dried  at 50 ◦C for 72 h in a vacuum oven. The films prepared in this study  included the following compositions: a neat PHBV film obtained by  extrusion of 500 g of dried PHBV; a binary blend of PHBV/PHB containing 400 g of PHBV and 100 g of PHB (20 % PHB); a binary blend of  475 g of PHBV with 25 g of BC-TOCN-AMD C-18; and a ternary blend  composed of 375 g of PHBV, 100 g of PHB, and 25 g of BC-TOCN-AMD C-  18. These films were maintained under vacuum to characterize their  mechanical and barrier properties. To avoid agglomeration and minimize possible adverse effects on PHBV films, the concentration of BC-  TOCN-AMD C-18 was set at 5 wt %, as suggested by prior research  (Ashori et al., 2019; Martínez-Sanz et al., 2014; Rastogi &amp; Samyn, 2020;  Sanchez-Garcia et al., 2008).

Film thickness was measured using a Mitutoyo Digimatic IP65 digital  micrometer (Mitutoyo, Kawasaki, Japan). Color and transparency were  determined using a 3NH colorimeter spectrophotometer model YS3020,  in accordance with ISO 13,468–1:2020. For this purpose, ΔE (difference  color) and the transmittance value were estimated from the reflectance  measured for each thin film, considering a standard D65 illuminant.  These tests were taken in 10 replicates throughout each film.

### 2.9. Mechanical properties

### 2.11. Film morphology and hydrophobicity

Tests of mechanical properties, such as tensile strength for the plastic  films, were conducted at 20 ± 0.5 ◦C and 80 ± 0.2 % relative humidity  (RH) following ASTM D638–14. The test involved films measuring  10×100 mm, with a thickness ranging from approximately 500 to 900  µm (standard thickness). A Shimadzu AG-IS 5KN universal testing machine, equipped with a 50 N load cell and calibrated according to Certificate No 21,463 issued by ICCLAB (Calibration Laboratory), was used  for mechanical tests. Low-strain tests were performed in four replicates  at a test speed of 12.5 mm/min.

The surface of the extruded films was inspected at a scale of 0.5 mm  under white light using a stereoscope. Additionally, scanning electron  microscopy (SEM) was employed for a more detailed analysis, particularly for cross-sectional imaging. For this, 1 cm x 0.5 mm sections of the  samples were introduced into epoxy resin (A/B) in the form of a cylinder. After drying, cuts were made with a diamond blade to obtain discs  of 0.5 cm in diameter and 1.0 mm thick. Likewise, both the disks and the  films were mounted on carbon strips and coated with gold by sputtering  to achieve a coating thickness of approximately 4.0 nm. Images were  taken at 15 kV with magnifications ranging from 200 to 1000X and a  working distance of 8.0 to 12 mm.

### 2.10. Oxygen and vapor permeability measurements

On the other hand, the degree of water repellency of each sample was  determined according to ASTM D7490, which involves CA measurements. Thin films, 8 × 8 cm, were used in triplicate for the tests at 20 ◦C,  utilizing distilled water and a modified goniometer. Droplets of water  (10 µL) were placed on the films and allowed to equilibrate for 30 s.  Droplet behavior was monitored at 0, 30, 60, 300, 600, and 1200 s  1800s.

The water vapor permeability coefficient (QH2O, g.mm/m2.atm.d)  and the water vapor transmission rate (WVTR, g/m2. d) were measured  according to ASTM E96–16. A known amount of distilled water (8.0 ± 0.5 g) was deposited in an aluminum cylinder; then, a 35 mm diameter  sample sheet was placed and sealed (effective permeation area of 9.7 × 10− 4 m2). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate at 20 ◦C and 60 % RH.  The water weight loss was evaluated over 7 days until constant values  (Castellanos et al., 2016).

### 2.12. Statistical analysis

The permeability of gaseous oxygen (QO2, g.mm/m2.atm.d) through  plastic sheets and coextruded materials was evaluated according to  ASTM E-2945 using a static method and hermetically sealed 10 cm  diameter cells. Each sheet was positioned in the grease seal, and a  fluorescence sensor was installed inside the film. The film was then  purged with N2  at a rate of 6 mL/min until the oxygen reading was  &lt;0.05 %. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate at 20 ◦C and 60 % RH.  The oxygen percentage reading was evaluated over 7 days using a  Mocon OpTech O2  Model P Gas Analyser, which is used as a non-  destructive headspace oxygen gas analyzer for packages.

Statistical analysis was performed using R software and analysis of  variance (ANOVA). Differences between means were evaluated by  Tukey’s test (HSD) at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05, using simultaneous  confidence intervals for multiple comparisons.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. BC-based hydrophobic derivatives synthesis and characterization

Fig. 1 illustrates the macroscopic and microscopic transformations in  BC occurring during the production of the cellulose-based hydrophobic  additive. Fig. 1a depicts the raw BC, while Fig. 1b illustrates the oxidized  BC-TOCN produced via TEMPO oxidation. Fig. 1c shows the amidation  of BC-TOCN; the inset demonstrates that the BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 phase  migrates to the surface of the reaction mixture and separates from water,  indicating a significant increase in hydrophobicity. Fig. 1d illustrates the  oxidation reaction step in which the primary hydroxyl group on the C6  carbon position of the anhydroglucose unit is oxidized to a carboxylic  acid,  which is  subsequently coupled  with an amine  group. Using  conductimetric titrations, we quantified BC’s degree of oxidation (DO)  before and after the TEMPO reaction. Initially, BC had a DO of 0.035  (0.21 mmol COOH/g of cellulose), which increased to 0.26 (1.53 mmol  COOH/g of cellulose) after oxidation. The increase in carboxylate  groups in BC-TOCN confirms successful oxidation, which paves the way  for modification via amidation. Previous studies using plant-derived  cellulose reported DO’s of 0.21 (Ovalle-Serrano et al., 2018), 0.29  (Calderon-Vergara et al., 2020´ ), and 0.25 (Li et al., 2023) using TEMPO  oxidation, while Okita et al. (2010) obtained a DO of 0.25 for BC-TOCN,  similar to the value found in this study. Additionally, the colloidal stability of BC-TOCN, as determined by ζ potential measurements, yielded  a value of − 61 mV. For oxidized nanocellulose samples,  ζ  potential  values below − 30 mV indicate good colloidal stability. This stability is  attributed to the presence of negatively charged carboxylate groups  (COO− ), which enhance electrostatic repulsion between nanofibers,  thereby preventing their aggregation and sedimentation (Mahendran &amp;  Ranjitha Kumari, 2016). This finding aligns with similar studies, where  the ζ potential values of oxidized cellulose nanofibers ranged from − 51  to − 75 mV (Calderon-Vergara et al., 2020´ ; Li et al., 2023; Okita et al.,  2010; Ovalle-Serrano et al., 2018).

Fig. 1. Macroscopic images and SEM micrographs showing the changes in BC upon processing: a) raw BC; b) BC-TOCN; c) BC-TOCN-AMD C-18; d) reaction scheme,  where R indicates n-octadecane.

The theoretical O:C ratio for pure cellulose is 0.833, whereas we  observed a slightly lower value of 0.79 for BC (Table 1). This difference  can likely be attributed to the presence of nitrogenous impurities, such  as cellular debris or remnants of the culture medium, as shown in the  SEM micrographs in Fig. 1a (Saavedra-Sanabria et al., 2021), which  constitute 3.28 % of the elemental weight of the sample. Furthermore,  the NaClO pre-treatment used in the cleaning process may have induced  slight oxidative degradation of the material, adding to this variation. (da  Cruz Nizer et al., 2020). The elemental composition of BC measured in  this study aligns with prior findings. Specifically, BC produced by a  symbiotic consortium of Medusomyces gisevii, Gluconacetobacter  sp.,  Acetobacter sp., and Zygosaccharomyces sp., exhibited a carbon content of  44.2 % and an oxygen content of 49.8 %, similar to the values observed  in this study (Pogorelova et al., 2020). Following TEMPO oxidation,  oxygen content increased from 46.52 % in raw BC to 61.61 % in  BC-TOCN due to the transformation of primary alcohols into carboxyl  groups. The nitrogen content dropped to 0.22 % in the oxidized cellulose. This decrease is likely due to the decomposition of organic tissue  caused by the high-oxidant reaction mixture, which generated soluble  residual compounds that were eliminated during the BC-TOCN cleaning  processes (Estrela et al., 2002). Conversely, in BC-TOCN-AMD C-18, the  nitrogen content increases to 3.57 %, confirming the effective grafting of  amide chains onto the BC-TOCN surface. This increase aligns with  findings from Hemraz et al. (2013), who reported cellulose nanocrystals  functionalized with 1,4-diaminobutane and 1,2-diaminoethane with  nitrogen contents of 2.1 % and 2.3 %, respectively. Furthermore, the  carbon  content  rose  from  33.36  %  in  BC-TOCN  to  62.78  %  in  BC-TOCN-AMD C-18. This increase in carbon content is attributed to the  incorporation of long aliphatic units into the C-18 amide groups. The  decrease in oxygen content in the amidated cellulose derivatives similarly occurs due to the formation of intermediate reactive esters during  the coupling reaction of aliphatic amines, which are the leaving groups  in the amidation process (Gomez et al., 2017´ ).

Raw BC typically features Gluconacetobacter xylinus cells on its surface because the polymer is secreted extracellularly by the microorganism (SEM image in Fig. 1a) (Saavedra-Sanabria et al., 2021). TEMPO  oxidation,  coupled  with  mechanical  defibrillation,  produces  a  three-dimensional network of individual nanofibers, characterized by  diameters ranging from 84 to 174 nanometers and lengths in the  micrometer scale, as observed in the SEM micrograph in Fig. 1b Similar  morphologies and dimensions have been documented; for instance,  Ovalle-Serrano et al. (2018) report diameters around 100 nm, while Liu  et al. (2016) identify diameters ranging from 200 nm to 1.5 μm for plant  cellulose nanofibers produced via TEMPO oxidation. The amidation of  BC-TOCN leads to a distinct separation of BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 from the  aqueous reaction matrix (Fig. 1c), emphasizing a notable enhancement  in the material’s hydrophobicity due to surface modifications. Amide  formation occurs via the removal of sodium ions from the surface carboxylic acid groups of BC-TOCN under basic conditions. The resulting  carboxylate anion then reacts with TBTU to form an active ester, which  readily reacts with the amino group to form amidated derivatives, as  illustrated in  Fig. 1d SEM images of amidated cellulose derivatives  reveal aggregates and continuous film layers, likely formed through  hydrophobic interactions between the surface-grafted amides on the  cellulose nanofibers. In contrast, hydrophilic BC-TOCN nanofibers easily  disperse in water, as illustrated in Fig. 1b, owing to the repulsive forces  created by negatively charged carboxylates. In BC-TOCN-AMD C-18, the  amidation removes these carboxylate groups, which diminishes electrostatic repulsion and leads to particle aggregation. This hydrophobic  characteristic is vital for adhesion, dispersibility in nonpolar matrices,  and biofilm formation (De-la-Pinta et al., 2019).

The IR profiles depicted in  Fig. 2a clearly describe the surface  chemistry of raw and modified materials. All samples exhibit the characteristic vibrational modes of cellulose. Notably, the broadband peak at  3341 cm− 1, which is present in all samples, is associated with the  stretching vibrations of the O–H bond in cellulose. The symmetric and  asymmetric C–O-C stretching vibrations of the β-glucosidic bond are  observed at 1159 and 1107 cm− 1, respectively. Additionally, the signals  at 1056 and 1031 cm− 1 are associated with skeletal vibrations involving  C–O stretching in cellulose’s pyranose ring structure (Dubey et al.,  2017;  Kondo  &amp;  Sawatari, 1996). Specific signals appear for various  materials. In BC-TOCN, the band at 1630 cm− 1 corresponds to bending  vibrations of the H–O-H bond in bound or structural water, which reflects the material’s highly hydrophilic nature. Additionally, the band at  1601 cm− 1 indicates COO⁻ stretching vibrations, confirming the presence  of  carboxylate  groups  from  the  sodium  salt  in  BC-TOCN  (Calderon-Vergara et al., 2020´ ;  Gomez et al., 2017´ ). A shoulder at  1632 cm− 1  in the same band suggests the overlap of aldehyde and  carbonyl groups with the COO⁻ stretching vibrations (Calderon-Vergara ´ et al., 2020). A small peak at 2882 cm− 1 is attributed to C–H stretching  vibrations in BC and BC-TOCN (Oh et al., 2005). The same signal increases dramatically for the amidated derivatives. In BC-TOCN-AMD  C-18, amidation results in a significant increase in the signals corresponding to the asymmetric stretching of methyl (–CH₃) and methylene  (–CH₂) groups at 2954 and 2850 cm− 1, respectively, reflecting the  presence of saturated alkyl chains. Additionally, the presence of the  amide group is confirmed by the overlapping broad bands of amide I and  amide II at 1643 and 1569 cm− 1, respectively (Gomez et al., 2017´ ;  Lasseuguette, 2008).

CrI increased from 77.86 % to 81.25 %  during cellulose nanofiber (CNF) synthesis and subsequent amidation  (A-CNF), likely due to the gradual removal of amorphous regions, according to Lu et al. (2021). Le Gars et al. (2020) argued that the rise in  CrI is probably related to the dissolution of part of the amorphous phase  during  the  grafting  process  in  esterification  reactions  of  cellulose  nanocrystals with fatty acids. Undoubtedly, eliminating the amorphous  regions and rearranging the crystalline region can increase CrI (Siti  Syazwani et al., 2022). Our findings support the idea that the increase in  CrI after amidation is due to improved crystallinity. Moreover, this  enhanced crystallinity may also play a role in boosting the tensile  strength of the amidated cellulose nanofibers, potentially reinforcing the  mechanical properties of polymeric matrices like PHAs as discussed  ahead (Ramli et al., 2015).

The crystallinity index (CrI) of BC, calculated using Eq. (3), is 70.22  %, which is comparable to the 79 % reported by Sampaio do Nascimento  et al. (2019) for BC produced by Komagataeibacter xylinus ATCC 53,582  via static fermentation. After TEMPO oxidation, the CrI decreases  slightly to 66.24 %. This change is attributed to the introduction of  carboxylate groups and the mechanical treatment involved in the production of nanofibers (Wang et al., 2012). As reported by Agustin et al.  (2018) mechanical fibrillation can cause cellulose depolymerization and  degradation, thereby altering its three-dimensional network. Similar  changes in CrI have been reported: from 75 % to 72 % in oil palm  bunches, from 72 % to 62 % in commercial microcrystalline cellulose,  and from 69 % to 65 % in bleached fique tow (Gomez et al., 2017´ ;  Ovalle-Serrano et al., 2018; Rohaizu &amp; Wanrosli, 2017) .

The thermal degradation profiles in Figs. 2c and 2d indicate that the  maximum decomposition temperature of the raw BC occurs at 342 ◦C,  accompanied by a 59 % mass loss. In contrast, BC-TOCN exhibits  maximum decomposition at 303 ◦C, accompanied by a 20 % mass loss,  indicating a decrease in thermal stability after oxidation. This reduced  thermal stability is due to the presence of carboxylic units, which promote the decomposition of BC by facilitating the transition from the  solid to the gas phase through decarboxylation reactions. At 232 ◦C, the  BC-TOCN TG curve shows a shoulder linked to a 14 % weight loss,  indicating the breakdown of carboxylic units on the material’s surface.  For BC-TOCN-AMD C-18, thermal decomposition starts between 140  and 210 ◦C, perhaps due to residual DMF release and primary amines  decomposition (Calderon-Vergara et al., 2020´ ). The thermal degradation  temperature  for  the  amidated  derivative  is  317  ◦C  for  BC-TOCN-AMD C-18, exceeding that of BC-TOCN. Decomposition above  400 ◦C is attributed to the combustion of excess carbonaceous residues,  yielding CO2, CO, and H2O.

A key characteristic of hydrophobic cellulose additives for polymeric  matrices is their surface-free energy. As anticipated, BC-TOCN shows a  significantly hydrophilic surface, with a maximum water CA of 42◦,  indicating its strong hydrophilic nature attributed to the polar groups  (–OH and –COOH) on its surface, as seen in Fig. 3. In contrast, the  amidated derivative BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 exhibits a maximum water CA  of 147◦ This indicates a shift towards highly hydrophobic surfaces due to  the inclusion of non-polar alkyl chains C-18 in the amidated derivatives.  Fig. 3 also illustrates the evolution of the water CA over time for BC-  TOCN  and  BC-TOCN-AMD  C-18.  Each  data  point  represents  the  average of the contact angle measured at the left (CAₗ) and right (CAᵣ)  sides of the deposited water droplet. This behaviour, especially in BC-TOCN-AMD C-18, may suggest  slow water absorption or interaction with surface functional groups.  This is particularly relevant for packaging applications requiring moisture regulation. Using the experimental CA measurements of the films with water and  diiodomethane, the SFE (γs) and its polar (γps) and dispersive (γds) components were calculated as summarized in Table 2. A SFE value of 63.08  mJ/m2 was measured for BC-TOCN, which is within the range of 66.32  to 45.13 mJ/m2 reported by Bashar et al. (2017), Calderon-Vergara et al. ´ (2020), and Sun et al. (2018) for cellulose nanocrystals and cellulose  nanofibers. In the BC-TOCN sample, the γps  component surpasses the γds  component  indicating  a  surface  where  polar  interactions  such  as  hydrogen bonding dominate. The SFE value for the BC-TOCN-AMD C-18  derivative drops significantly to 7.5 mJ/m2. We have previously reported a SFE value of 10.17 mJ/m² for amidated nanocellulose derived  from plants (Calderon-Vergara et al., 2020´ ). After surface modification  the  γpscomponent in BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 decreased drastically (0.38  mJ/m2) to a lower level than the γds component (7.13 mJ/m2) indicating  the predominance of hydrophobic and van der Waals-type interactions.  This decrease parallels previous findings where a reduction in SFE to 13  mJ/m2 was observed after silane modification, attributed to dispersive  interactions characteristic of hydrophobic materials with minimal contributions from γps  component and low surface reactivity (Hasan et al.,  2019). When compared to findings from studies from the scientific  literature, the SFE values and its components correspond to the values  reported for cellulose materials both prior to and following hydrophobization. For instance, a decrease in SFE from 66.14 mJ/m2 to 13.93  mJ/m2  was  reported  for  cellulose  nanofibers  following  hydrophobization with trichloro(1H,1H,2H,2H-heptadecafluorodecyl) silane  (THFS) (Bashar et al., 2017). The low SFE values indicate that these  materials are ideal for applications that require superhydrophobic  surfaces.

The surface modification of BC focused on improving compatibility  with the PHBV polymeric matrix, and the results show that BC-TOCN-  AMD C-18 could affect PHA films surface properties. A CA of 147◦ indicates that the SFE is predominantly influenced by the γds component  and BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 may modulate the surface hydrophobicity of  PHA-based films when used as an additive. This behavior stems from the  aliphatic character of the AMN C-18 primary amine, consisting of a  linear hydrocarbon chain attached to the BC-TOCN framework. Consequently, these films will likely absorb less water, which is crucial for  extending the film’s storage life and usability. Reduced water absorption  is essential as it slows down the natural degradation process of packaging materials both before and during use.

### 3.2. Structural and optical properties of PHBV and PHBV/PHB films  containing BC-TOCN-AMD C-18

Films 2 m long and 30 cm wide were produced by extrusion using  PHBV, a PHBV/PHB blend (20 wt %), a PHBV/ BC-TOCN-AMD C-18  blend (5 wt %), and a PHBV/PHB (20 wt %)/ BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 (5 wt  %). All extruded films had average thicknesses ranging from 70 to 90  µm, a standard accepted in the packaging industry (Perez-Martinez  et al., 2024). The reference PHBV-only film exhibits a milky appearance  and a textured surface (Fig. 4a), whereas the addition of PHB yields a  yellow coloration to the films (Fig. 4b). Micrographs and SEM images of  the PHBV/PHB blend show a reduction in pore size on the film surface  (Fig. 4e-f) compared to neat PHBV. Based on previous research, this is  due to PHB molecules influencing crystallization in the PHBV matrix. In  this case, the low-molecular-weight PHB could disrupt the characteristic  high-molecular-weight PHBV crystallization kinetics associated with  laminar structures and small spherulitic sites. (Majerczak  &amp;  Liggat,  2023; Pracella et al., 2021; Rivera-Briso&amp; Serrano-Aroca, 2018).

Fig. 3. Evolution of the water contact angle over time for BC-TOCN and BC-  TOCN-AMD C-18. Each data point represents the average contact angle from  the left and right sides of a single droplet (CAₗ, CAᵣ). Error bars indicate the  standard deviation of these three measurements.

Film coloration is preserved in the fourth formulation, which contains both PHAs (PHBV and PHB) and exhibits a notable color difference  from the reference film. This particular film, PHBV/PHB/BC-TOCN-  AMD C-18, displayed a milky yellow hue, which previous researchers  have linked to the use of derivatized cellulose additives in PHA-based  polymeric films (Doineau et al., 2022). However, our results suggest  that the yellowish hue of the PHBV/PHB/BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 film may  have stemmed from residual traces of the culture media used during the  production of PHB from cacao waste. This is confirmed because the ΔE  parameter with PHBV/BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 is &lt;5, indicating that PHB is  responsible for the coloration in the other two films.

The transparency of the films varied in terms of light transmittance (  %), indicating that BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 introduces elements that reduce  light transmission by 12.17 % and 14.23 % compared to the PHBV/BC-  TOCN-AMD C-18 and PHBV/PHB/BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 films, respectively. This reduction is due to the increased size and number of aggregates compared to the reference film (Fig. 4c-d), as observed by  Panaitescu et al. (2020) in mixtures of 3-PHB reinforced with cellulose  fibers from wood.

Conversely, the nucleating impact of modified cellulose on the  PHBV/BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 film was evident (Fig. 4c-g). The material  appears continuous and transparent at the macroscopic level. Still, it  exhibits a heterogeneous surface composed of well-defined polymer/  additive areas, approximately 60 µm in size, at the microscopic level  (Fig. 4c). SEM micrographs show a mix of smooth and rough regions on  the  PHBV/BC-TOCN-AMD  C-18  film  surface.  This  indicates  that,  although  the  amidated  cellulose  nanofibers  are  evenly  spread  throughout the film, they fail to integrate effectively with the PHBV.  However, the smooth areas surrounding the amidated nanocellulose  aggregates exhibit a rapid crystallization effect, indicating that amidated nanocellulose can accelerate PHBV nucleation, enhance crystallization kinetics, and form small crystals that produce smooth films  (Fig. 4g), (Carvalho Benini et al., 2020; Ten et al., 2010). However,  aggregates create weak points that affect the film’s mechanical properties and permeability, as will be shown later.

### 3.3. Mechanical properties of PHBV and PHBV/PHB films containing  BC-TOCN-AMD C-18

Our observations indicate that the blend PHBV/PHB/BC-TOCN-AMD  C-18 produces smooth films where, at the microscopic level, all components are homogeneously distributed. The cellulose-based additive  enhances compatibility among the polymers, promotes nucleation sites,  and increases crystallization rates, resulting in smaller crystal formations rather than larger spherulites. Additives for polymers in food  packaging are highly valued for enhancing the dispersion and distribution of polymer blend components, which results in homogeneous films  with minimal agglomeration and acceptable optical properties. Nonetheless, their impact on the blend’s mechanical properties is also crucial.

Fig. 5 displays the stress-strain curves for PHBV, PHBV/PHB, PHBV/  BC-TOCN–C18,  and  PHBV/PHB/BC-TOCN–C18.  The  mechanical  behavior of the samples resembles that of flexible plastic extruded  laminates. The PHBV sample shows an elastic limit of about 0.5 MPa and  exhibits  the  plastic  deformation  typical  of  the  polymer  (248  %)  (Melendez-Rodriguez et al., 2021; Soares da Silva et al., 2023). Adding  PHB to PHBV (Fig. 5, red line) results in a higher maximum stress (3.6  MPa), making the film less elastic with an elongation of 203 %. The  increase in stress is due to changes in stress transfer within the matrix, as  PHB increases the crystallinity of the blend, thereby enhancing tensile  strength compared to PHBV (Sudesh et al., 2000). Additionally, as  proposed by Chen and Wu (2005) because both polymers contain PHB  chains, their interaction becomes more compatible, improving stress  transfer under higher loads. This combination provides two key characteristics to the film: stiffness from PHB and ductility from PHBV,  resulting in a mechanically stronger film,as described by Bugnicourt  et al. (2014).

The incorporation of low molecular weight PHB (20 wt %) into  PHBV/BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 blends produces a whitish film at the  macroscopic scale, while revealing a homogeneous and smooth surface  at the microscopic scale, as illustrated in Fig. 4d The SEM micrographs in  Fig. 4h indicate improved dispersion and distribution of BC-TOCN-AMD  C-18 throughout the film, resulting in a significant decrease in aggregate  formation. This observation highlights the compatibilizing influence of  low-molecular-weight PHB, as its presence affects the mobility of the  PHBV chains, thereby enhancing the interaction between the two PHAs  and the amidated cellulose nanofibers (Lee et al., 2014). Still, a balance  and limit in the concentrations of the components must be considered  (Meereboer et al., 2020).

Continuing to evaluate how different components affect the physical  and optical properties of extruded films, measurements of film thickness, light transmittance, and color difference (ΔE) were performed.  These parameters were analyzed to evaluate the suitability of the materials for applications where visual transparency and appearance are  relevant, such as in packaging. Using the PHBV as a reference, Table 3 presents the thickness and optical characteristics of the extruded films.  Although the PHBV-only film exhibits a milky appearance, it maintains  good transparency, allowing light to pass through. Adding PHB to the  PHBV matrix does not result in significant differences in light transmission (Tukey test). Furthermore, the overall color difference (ΔE)  indicates that the colorimetric distinctions are notable (ΔE ≥ 5). Using  this criterion, the results show that the addition of 20 % PHB to PHBV  (ΔE: 6.0) yields a color shift toward more milky, brown films. There are  also significant differences in films containing the PHB polymer, as  indicated by statistical parameters, compared to the samples without  PHB. This is related to the fact that PHB still contains traces of components from the biomass used in its synthesis, which contribute to the  coloration (Fig. 4b).

Incorporating BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 into PHBV (Fig. 5, blue line)  leads to a 77 % decrease in deformation and an increase in stress when  compared to the PHBV film. This effect could be attributed to the poor  dispersion of the nanocellulose additive in the film, where agglomerations create high-energy areas that lead to material failure at lower  deformations (Driscoll et al., 2016). On the other hand, the increase in  the elastic zone, up to 0.9 MPa, indicates that amidated nanocellulose as  an additive enables the film to withstand significant stress before  experiencing irreversible deformation. Previous reports have shown that  PHA films fail under low deformations when additivated with cellulose  nanodisks (Martínez-Sanz et al., 2014). This emphasizes the need to  optimize cellulose interactions with the polymer matrix to improve its  mechanical properties (Gurunathan et al., 2015). Without proper optimization, the agglomerates caused by cellulose-based additives often act  as defects or stress points, reducing the mechanical performance of the  films (Cao et al., 2016). Therefore, researchers such as Ma et al. (2023) suggest that an optimal level of 5 wt % cellulose additive is necessary to  boost mechanical strength. Higher levels may lead to aggregation, which  negatively impacts film performance.

On the other hand, the PHBV/PHB/BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 film exhibits significant improvement in mechanical properties compared to  films  produced  with  different  blends.  The  elastic  limit  increases  considerably, reaching up to 5.4 MPa (Fig. 5, green line), while the ultimate tensile strength decreases slightly compared to PHBV. Strain  hardening and necking are present in the sample, allowing the fracture  point to be extended to the highest value of all tested samples. These  observations suggest a synergistic effect among the blend components,  enhancing compatibility and surface interaction for improved dispersion  and distribution (see Fig. 4h). As a result, the elastic properties of the  films extruded from the blend improved. We hypothesize that the  introduction of low-MW PHB, with smaller crystalline segments, promotes a continuum and homogeneous blend between the components.  Additionally, the capacity to endure high stresses during deformation  makes films created from the PHBV/PHB/BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 blend  excellent candidates for packaging applications. When comparing the  stress (5.5 MPa) and strain (193 %) features of the PHBV/ PHB/BC-  TOCN-AMD C-18 film with synthetic polymers like polyethylene (PP,  6–26 MPa and 200–600 %) and polypropylene (PE, 10–20 MPa and  200–800 %), the elongation and tensile strength of the biopolymer blend  are comparable to these synthetic polymers commonly used in packaging (Michiels et al., 2017).

As expected from the  morphology observed by microscopy, the presence of short chains of  PHB in the PHBV/PHB film effectively reduces minimally the vapor  transmission rate by 0.5 g/m2 d. On the other hand, the incorporation of  amide cellulose generated an increase in WVTR, QH2O, and QO2 in the  PHBV/BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 film, which is attributed to factors such as  low surface homogeneity, incompatibility, and differences in the number and size of pores, as shown in the SEM micrographs (Fig. 4c-g).

The existence of surface defects and fissures enhances H₂O and O₂  transport through the film, as was reported by Malmir et al. (2017).  Cellulose-based additives can affect the barrier properties of PHAs, as  demonstrated by  Seoane et al. (2017), who observed a decrease in  WVTR when 6 % nanocellulose was added to PHB, indicating that the  material enhances the moisture barrier of the polymer an essential effect  in many packaging applications (Driscoll et al., 2016). Conversely, Dhar  et al. (2015) reported an increased oxygen transmission rate with a 5 wt  % nanocellulose loading in a P3HB polymeric matrix. This finding  contrasts with Seoane’s observations (Seoane et al., 2017), emphasizing  the need to optimize the additive’s dispersion within the polymer matrix  to strike a suitable balance between the material´s mechanical and barrier properties (Dhar et al., 2015).

The  PHBV/PHB/BC-TOCN-AMD  C-18  film  exhibited  the  lowest  water transmission and water/oxygen permeability values among all  samples tested. Consistent with the data on mechanical properties, the  addition of amidated nanocellulose and PHB synergistically enhances  the compatibility of the blend components, thereby improving the film’s  barrier properties. For example, in the PHBV/PHB/BC-TOCN-AMD C-18  film, oxygen and water vapor permeability decreased by 9.54 % and  10.47 %, respectively, compared to PHBV. This reduction brings the  permeability values close to those of synthetic polymers such as PP and  LDPE. In conclusion, the enhanced compatibility of the blend components promotes films with improved distribution and dispersion, as well  as enhanced mechanical and barrier properties. According to the literature, the permeability properties of the polymeric films directly rely on  their homogeneity and indirectly on humidity and the production  method, such as solvent casting or extrusion (Popa et al., 2022).

Film hydrophobicity is another crucial parameter in packaging,  indicating how the surface interacts with liquids such as water. Time-  lapse hydrophobicity measurements were conducted over 30 min to  evaluate the films’  wetting resistance (Fig. 6). All samples exhibited  hydrophobic characteristics within 2 min, with CA exceeding 90◦ (Gianangelo &amp; Holst, 2015). The PHBV/PHB film exhibits a slight increase in contact angle (CA) by 5◦, suggesting less interaction with water  and confirming that PHB improves the homogeneity of the PHBV film.  When  amidated  nanocellulose  is  added,  the  CA  of  the  PHBV/BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 film rises above 99◦, although the presence  of agglomerates causes this effect to be uneven throughout the film.  Once  the  components’  compatibility  improves,  the  PHBV/PHB/BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 film achieves a maximum CA of 112◦,  indicating a homogeneous and well-organized surface that resists water  penetration (Arrieta, Lopez et al., 2015´ ). During the 30-minute measurement, all films exhibited a continuous decline in water wettability;  however, the PHBV/PHB/BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 film consistently kept  the highest contact angle.

### 3.4. Barrier properties and hydrophobicity of PHBV and PHBV/PHB  films containing BC-TOCN-AMD C-18

Earlier studies have found that the CA decreases as the unmodified  nanocellulose content in aliphatic polyesters, such as PHB, increases  (Arrieta,  Fortunati  et  al.,  2015).  The  authors  attribute  the  film’s  increased wettability to the hydrophilic nature of the cellulose additive,  which tends to accumulate on the surface of the polymer matrix as its  concentration rises (Panaitescu et al., 2020). As a result, our findings  highlight the need to modify cellulose surfaces to enhance hydrophobicity, improve PHA blend compatibility, and adjust the hydrophobicity  of  PHA-based  films.  Films  made  from  blends  like  PHBV/PHB/BC-TOCN-AMD  C-18  might  be  well-suited  for  food  packaging where controlling humidity is crucial (Heimowska et al.,  2017; Qasim et al., 2020).

## 4. Conclusions

The effective formation of carboxylic units on the bacterial cellulose  surface enabled the coupling of primary amines (AMN C-18) and the  modulation of the material’s hydrophobicity. These changes are evident  in the new signals in the FT-IR spectra, which are attributed to the  formation of hydrocarbon chains and amide bonds, as well as the increase in nitrogen content determined by elemental analysis. On the  other hand, morphological and dimensional changes were observed on  the surface of both raw and oxidized BC, demonstrating that the TEMPO  reaction, combined with ultrasound, effectively leads to the separation  and effective dispersion of the nanofibers. Furthermore, due to amidation, BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 exhibits increased crystallinity, resulting in  greater rigidity that reinforces the mechanical properties of the polymeric matrix. Similarly, the hydrophobization of the material was  confirmed, as evidenced by a CA of 147◦ after AMN C-18 coupling,  which is up to three times higher than that observed in BC-TOCN.  Likewise, the reduction of the SFE from 63.08 mJ/m² in BC-TOCN to  7.5 mJ/m² in BC-TOCN-AMD C-18 is consistent with an increase in hydrophobicity after amidation of the oxidized nanocellulose.

Composite films of pure PHBV, PHBV/PHB (20 wt %), PHBV/BC-  TOCN-AMD C-18 (5 wt %), and PHBV/PHB (20 wt %)/BC-TOCN-AMD  C-18 (5 wt %) were produced by extrusion. The surface modification of  BC contributed to an increase in the hydrophobicity of the PHBV films as  well as to a decrease in their transparency, likely due to the high crystallinity of BC-TOCN-AMD C-18. On the other hand, adding 20 wt %  PHB favored the formation of a more homogeneous and smoother surface, reducing the agglomerated areas. This effect, related to ordering  the small chains of PHB, led to a decrease in the wettability of the films, a  marked increase in opacity, a significant reinforcement of the mechanical properties, and a slight decrease in gas permeability. Our research  shows that PHBV films containing low-molecular-weight PHB and an  amidated nanocellulose additive - both sourced from byproducts of the  Colombian cacao industry - have mechanical and barrier properties that  hold great promise for sustainable packaging.
